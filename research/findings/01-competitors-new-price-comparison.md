Research date: 2026-11-17.

## 1. Bottom line

1. Price comparison for new goods in Australia is a crowded, low-margin commodity. Zyft (claims 50,000+ retailers, raised A$7.5m in April 2025) and BuyWisely (20,000+ retailers, price-history graphs) already do "search an item, see every retailer's price, get an alert" for free. (Confidence: medium-high; search.)
2. The best-funded Australian attempt at a consumer deal-finding app, Little Birdie (A$30m from Commonwealth Bank), paused in May 2024, was sold to Cashrewards in August 2024, and closed for good with Cashrewards on 8 September 2025. No official reason was published; press reports say Cashrewards missed ANZ's expectations. (Confidence: high on the dates, medium on the reason.)
3. Google is the real incumbent and it is moving fast in Australia. Price tracking is available in Australia per Google's help page, and Australia became Google's first Asia-Pacific market for agentic checkout in mid-June 2026, with Bunnings, Kogan, THE ICONIC, Adore Beauty and Petbarn. (Confidence: medium-high; trade press, not Google's own page.)
4. The other AI assistants are behind in Australia on buying, not on answering. ChatGPT's Instant Checkout never launched here and OpenAI scaled it back in March 2026; Perplexity's Instant Buy is US-only; Amazon's assistant (Rufus, renamed Alexa for Shopping in May 2026) has no confirmed Australian launch. Any Australian can still ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Perplexity "what's the best price for X" and get a web-searched answer.
5. I found no Australian product, and no general AI assistant, that puts new prices and second-hand listings side by side and gives a verdict on a used asking price. The closest thing globally is Phia (US, fashion, about US$43.5m raised), which shows resale alternatives next to retail prices. Booko does new-versus-used for books only.
6. So the commodity half of the founder's idea (find the cheapest new price) is already free and good enough. The unsolved half is exactly the founder's own story: "is this used asking price a good deal against what it costs new?"
7. The main risk is not an Australian startup; it is Google or OpenAI adding used listings and a verdict. I found no evidence either has done so, but I could not test it directly.
8. Business model warning: every incumbent lives on affiliate commissions or merchant pay-per-click. Second-hand listings on Facebook Marketplace and Gumtree pay no commission, so the unsolved half is also the half that is hardest to monetise.

Points that matter:

- **CHOICE tested the three main trackers (March 2025).** Zyft's prices were most likely to be current; BuyWisely and Price Hipster lagged; BuyWisely put Amazon results first regardless of price. Stale prices are a documented weakness of the whole category. (Confidence: medium-high; search summary of CHOICE.)
- **Zyft is the one to study.** Founded 2019 by iSelect founder Damien Waller; claims 550,000 users and 100,000 searches a day; valued at A$37.5m in the April 2025 round. Revenue is not public; one third-party estimate of US$1.4m is unreliable. A reported A$37.5m valuation after six years is a signal of how small this market is.
- **New small entrants keep appearing** (Findfetcher and others publish "best price tracker Australia 2026" posts ranking themselves). Barriers to entry are low and nobody has broken out.
- **None show second-hand listings** except Booko for books. I found no Australian comparison service that includes Gumtree, Facebook Marketplace or eBay used items. (Confidence: medium; absence of evidence from searches, not a hands-on check.)

What this means:

- For **new goods**, a free general assistant already does a rough version of the founder's 50–100 minute retailer check in a minute or two, with known price-accuracy problems. That is the baseline the app must beat on day one.
- For **second-hand**, I found no evidence that any general assistant searches Facebook Marketplace or Gumtree. That is an inference from product descriptions, not a test. Facebook Marketplace needs a login and blocks crawlers (background), which would explain it.
- A **structured verdict** exists only for new goods and only as price history ("this is low for the last 12 months"). Nobody found tonight answers "is this used price fair against new?"

### Q4. Coverage test — fishing rod and reel combo

**Not performed.** I could not open BCF, any comparison site, or any assistant, so I did not pick a model or record results. I am not going to guess what they return.

What I can say from evidence: Zyft and BuyWisely claim tens of thousands of retailers, so BCF is probably indexed, but Zyft's own listing says not all retailers are covered and CHOICE found lagging prices. Combos are a hard case for any comparison engine because a rod-plus-reel bundle is often a retailer-specific product code, so matching across stores is weak (inference).

Protocol for the founder, about 30 minutes: pick one combo on bcf.com.au with a manufacturer model number; search that exact name in Zyft, BuyWisely, Price Hipster, Getprice, Google Shopping, ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity; record for each whether BCF appears, how many other Australian retailers appear, whether the price matches the retailer's site, and whether any used listing shows up. Then ask each assistant: "It's $X used on Marketplace, is that a good deal?"

### Q6. Commodity versus unsolved

**Commodity (free, several providers):** multi-retailer price search for new goods; price-drop alerts; price history (BuyWisely, Price Hipster, Keepa); barcode scan (Zyft, Booko); browser extension; conversational product research; affiliate monetisation.

**Rare or absent in everything I found:**
- New and second-hand results in one view (only Phia for fashion in the US, Booko for books).
- A verdict on a used asking price relative to new price, condition and age.
- Facebook Marketplace or Gumtree coverage.
- Local pickup versus shipped as a first-class comparison, with distance and postage in the total cost.
- Reliable real-time price accuracy (CHOICE found lag in all three trackers).
- Matching bundles and near-equivalent models rather than exact product codes.
- Sold-price data for used goods rather than asking prices.

## 3. Implications for the product

- **Do not build a new-goods price comparison engine.** Zyft, BuyWisely and Google already give that away, and Little Birdie shows A$30m does not buy a durable position.
- **Lead with the verdict on a used listing.** "Paste a Marketplace or Gumtree listing, get the new price across Australian retailers and a fair-price judgement" is the founder's own problem and is the gap in every table above.
- **Treat new-price lookup as an input, not the product.** Buy it or borrow it (retailer feeds, a search API, or an LLM with web search) and expect it to be imperfect on freshness.
- **Assume Google and OpenAI will keep improving new-goods answers in Australia.** Anything defensible has to sit where they are weak: logged-in second-hand marketplaces, local pickup, sold-price history, and categories with messy product matching such as fishing, tools, bikes and baby gear.
- **Plan monetisation before building.** Affiliate income only arrives when the user buys new, which gives the app a financial reason to steer people away from used. That conflict needs an answer (subscription, per-check fee, or eBay's affiliate programme for used items).
- **Avoid the browser-extension affiliate pattern** in light of the Honey fallout.
