# Track 3 — Data access and legal feasibility

Output: `research/findings/03-data-access-and-legal.md`

## Goal
Determine, source by source, how the app could lawfully and reliably get listing and price data, and what the legal and platform-policy risks are. "Scan the entire internet" is the founder's phrase; this track turns it into what is actually obtainable. This track decides feasibility more than any other, so precision matters more than optimism.

## Questions
1. **Source-by-source access table.** For each source below, report: official API or feed (name, access requirements, cost, rate limits, commercial-use and comparison-site restrictions, whether Australian marketplace data is covered), affiliate programme, what the terms of service say about automated access, known enforcement history, and a practical rating (open / gated but obtainable / hostile).
   - New, Australia: Amazon AU, eBay AU, Kogan, Catch, JB Hi-Fi, Harvey Norman, The Good Guys, Officeworks, Bunnings, BCF, Anaconda, Rebel, Supercheap Auto, Big W, Kmart, Target, Myer, David Jones, Decathlon AU, and Shopify-based independents.
   - Used and peer-to-peer: Facebook Marketplace, Gumtree, eBay AU used and sold listings, Cash Converters, Carousell, Depop, Reebelo, Back Market, Grays, Lloyds Auctions, local pawn and resale chains.
   - Aggregated feeds: Google Merchant Center / Shopping (including the Content API and any product search API), Bing Shopping, affiliate networks active in Australia (Commission Factory, Awin, Impact, Rakuten, Partnerize, CJ) and their product datafeeds, commercial product-data APIs and SERP providers (for example SerpApi, Oxylabs, Bright Data, Zyte, DataForSEO, PriceAPI), and Schema.org product markup on retailer pages.
2. **Facebook Marketplace in depth.** This is the source in the founder's own story and the hardest. Is there any official route to listing data (Marketplace Partner API, commerce partnerships, the eBay–Meta arrangement)? What happened in Meta v. Bright Data (2024) and any later cases? What do Meta's terms say? What are the non-scraping alternatives: user pastes a link, share-sheet extension, screenshot plus vision model, in-app browser where the user is logged in themselves, user-side browser extension? How do Phia, Beni, Swoopa and similar tools get their data? State clearly which options are lawful and durable and which are fragile.
3. **Law.** Australia first: contract (terms of service) claims, Copyright Act and database protection, the Criminal Code computer offences, Privacy Act and the 2024–2026 reforms as they apply to seller data in listings, Australian Consumer Law and the ACCC's guidance for comparison websites (disclosure of commercial relationships, ranking, coverage claims), and spam and consumer data rules. Then the main US and EU reference points (hiQ v. LinkedIn, Van Buren, Meta v. Bright Data, X Corp v. Bright Data, Ryanair v. PR Aviation, EU Database Directive, GDPR) to the extent they inform risk and later expansion. Flag where a qualified lawyer must be consulted. This is research, not legal advice — say so in the output.
4. **AI agents and browsing on a user's behalf.** What is the current legal and policy position on an AI agent visiting sites for a user (for example Amazon v. Perplexity over the Comet browser in 2025, and any outcome since)? Does that route change the risk profile?
5. **App store policy.** Apple App Store and Google Play rules that bear on this app: scraping, in-app browsers, affiliate links, use of third-party trademarks and content, AI-generated advice.
6. **Recommended data strategy.** Tier the sources: what can be covered at launch through official and affiliate routes, what needs a user-assisted route, and what should be left out. Estimate what share of a typical Australian shopper's relevant listings each tier would cover, with your reasoning and confidence.
